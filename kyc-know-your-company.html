What is Recruitment? What is Staffing?

These two words get used interchangeably outside the industry — inside it, they mean different things, and knowing the difference is the foundation of everything else you'll learn.

Recruitment

Recruitment is the process of finding, attracting, screening, interviewing, and selecting candidates for a job.

Staffing

Staffing is broader. It means determining the need for a role, recruiting the candidate, hiring them, onboarding them, and then managing workforce requirements over time — retaining and training the workforce as well.

Key difference: Recruitment focuses on finding and hiring talent. Staffing focuses on maintaining and managing the workforce, including recruitment as one part of that.

Chapter 3

The Staffing Ecosystem

You know who our clients are. Beyond HonorVet and the client, though, there's usually a layer of platforms and intermediaries in between — knowing these acronyms is how you understand where a requisition actually comes from.

Full-time hours & overtime

In the U.S., a full-time employee is generally expected to work 40 hours a week. If they work more than 40 hours in a week, that additional time is considered overtime (OT), typically paid at 1.5x the regular pay rate.

MSP — Managed Service Provider

An MSP is a third-party organization that manages a company's temporary, contract, and contingent workforce program — including staffing vendors, hiring processes, compliance, and workforce operations — on behalf of the client and the staffing agency/vendor.

Common MSPs you'll encounter: Randstad Sourceright, Allegis Global Solutions (AGS), Pontoon Solutions, Magnit, PeopleScout, KellyOCG, TAPFIN, CXC Global, Guidant Global, and Workspend.

MSP fees are always a percentage, charged on top of the engagement. As a rough guide: IT roles run 1–5%, Healthcare roles run 1–10%, and Non-IT roles run 1–5%.

Chapter 5

Pay Rate, Bill Rate, Markup & Tax Terms

Winning the account and staffing the req is only half the job. Next comes the number that decides whether it's actually worth doing: what the client pays HonorVet, and what HonorVet pays the candidate. This is the chapter you'll use most — getting bill rate, pay rate, and worker classification right is the difference between a compliant, profitable placement and a costly mistake.

Bill rate vs. pay rate

TermDefinition
Bill rateThe rate the client pays to HonorVet for the candidate's services
Pay rateThe rate HonorVet pays the candidate
Net bill rateThe bill rate after the MSP fee and other fixed deductions are factored in
Loaded costThe candidate's actual cost to HonorVet — pay rate plus the 25% overhead on top of it
Gross marginWhat HonorVet actually keeps: net bill rate minus loaded cost

That distinction between pay rate and loaded cost matters more than it looks. A candidate only cares about the pay rate they're offered. HonorVet has to think in loaded cost, because that 25% overhead — mainly the employer's share of payroll tax withholding, plus FICA and minor benefits/payroll costs — is real money going out the door on top of the paycheck, regardless of client or position.

Which formula you use next depends entirely on how the account is structured. HonorVet accounts run one of two ways, and they are genuinely different calculations — not the same rule in two outfits.

Bill-rate-driven accounts: protecting a dollar margin

Here, the client hands HonorVet a fixed bill rate, and the job is to negotiate a pay rate that still leaves real money on the table.

Loaded Cost = Pay Rate + (25% × Pay Rate)

Net Bill Rate = Bill Rate − MSP Fee

Gross Margin = Net Bill Rate − Loaded Cost

Say the bill rate is $110/hr with a 5% MSP fee, so the net bill rate is $104.50. Here's how three different pay rate offers play out:

Pay rate offeredOverhead (25%)Loaded costGross margin
$80$20.00$100.00$4.50
$85$21.25$106.25−$1.75 (a loss)
$75$18.75$93.75$10.75

This is exactly why a recruiter who only looks at "bill rate minus pay rate" gets fooled — $110 minus $80 looks like $30 of room, but the real gross margin is only $4.50 once the MSP fee and overhead are actually accounted for. If a Team Lead sets a margin target (say, at least $10/hr), this is the math that tells you what you can actually offer a candidate.

Markup-driven accounts: protecting a percentage

Here, the client sets a fixed markup percentage instead of a bill rate, and HonorVet builds the bill rate up from the pay rate.

Bill Rate = Pay Rate × (1 + Markup%)

Pay Rate = Bill Rate ÷ (1 + Markup%)

If a candidate's pay rate is $50/hr and the markup is 40%, the bill rate is $50 × 1.40 = $70/hr. That $20 difference is what covers HonorVet's overhead, taxes, benefits, and profit — all in one number this time, rather than broken out separately like the bill-rate-driven example above. It also works backward: given a bill rate and a target markup, that same formula tells you the most you can offer as a pay rate.

HonorVet rule: on markup-driven accounts, we do not work below a 33% markup — that's the floor in the Bill Rate = Pay Rate × (1 + Markup%) formula above. It doesn't apply the same way to bill-rate-driven accounts, which are governed by dollar-margin targets instead, as shown in the example above.

Tax terms: W-2, 1099, and C2C

How a candidate is paid changes who withholds taxes, who provides benefits, and how much risk HonorVet carries. There are three main arrangements.

W-2

The staffing agency hires the worker as an employee. Federal income tax, state income tax, Medicare tax, and Social Security tax — four types in total — are withheld directly from the paycheck. The worker receives a W-2 form at year end, and benefits like health insurance, PTO, or retirement plans may be offered. This is generally the lowest-risk classification.

Example: a software developer placed by HonorVet receives biweekly paychecks with taxes already deducted.

1099

The worker operates as an independent contractor / sole proprietor. No payroll taxes are withheld, and benefits are generally not provided. In our world, this arrangement most often applies to physicians.

C2C — Corp-to-Corp

The contractor works through their own business entity, usually an LLC or corporation. HonorVet contracts with that business, not the individual — the contractor's company invoices HonorVet and is responsible for its own payroll, taxes, insurance, and compliance. It offers more flexibility but comes with more administrative overhead.

Example: Jane owns an LLC. HonorVet signs a contract with Jane's LLC, and the LLC invoices HonorVet for Jane's services.

Minimum wage varies by state and role — always confirm the current applicable rate before quoting a pay rate to a candidate rather than relying on memory.

Chapter 6

Compliance, Immunization Records & Background Verification

You've settled the rate. Before any of it becomes real, the candidate still has to clear compliance — this is where a placement can fall apart fastest if it's rushed, so treat it as seriously as sourcing.

Work authorization compliance

I-9 verification — confirming identity and eligibility to work in the U.S. — is mandatory for every new hire, citizen and noncitizen alike. It has three sections: the employee completes Section 1, and the employer completes Section 2 and, when applicable, Section 3 (for reverification or rehire). Employers can be fined for a form that isn't completed properly, and a candidate can be prosecuted for knowingly entering false information — so this isn't a box-ticking step. HonorVet is also responsible for retaining every completed I-9 on file.

Commonly accepted documents: a U.S. passport on its own; or, without a passport, a Green Card (Permanent Resident Card with photo); or a driver's license / state-issued photo ID paired with a Social Security card if neither of the above is available.

Non-IT clients accept only USC (U.S. Citizen) or GC (Green Card) work authorization. GC-EAD — a pending Green Card work permit — is not valid for any Non-IT client. Always verify work authorization type before submitting a Non-IT candidate.

W-4

Form W-4 is the IRS form a new hire fills out to tell HonorVet how much tax to withhold from their paycheck, based on marital status, dependents, and other factors. It's paperwork every W-2 placement completes alongside the I-9.

Background verification (BGV)

A standard BGV package generally includes:

  • Criminal background check — typically county, state, and federal level, using an SSN trace to identify prior addresses, and often fingerprint-based rather than just name-based
  • Employment verification — confirming past employers, dates, and roles
  • Education, license, and certification verification — degree confirmation, state board license verification for nurses, and relevant professional certifications (for example, BLS/ACLS in healthcare)
  • OIG and SAM.gov exclusion checks — confirming the candidate isn't excluded from federal healthcare programs or federal contracting, which matters most for our government clients
  • Sex offender registry check
  • Professional reference checks
  • Drug screening (the "DT" in "BGC" — background check and drug test)

Reading a criminal record: felony vs. misdemeanor

A criminal background check can return charges at very different severity levels, and knowing the difference matters for reading a report correctly.

Felony

A serious crime, generally punishable by more than a year in prison — things like murder, robbery, or serious drug trafficking.

Misdemeanor

A less serious offense, generally punishable by a fine or up to a year in jail. Many states grade misdemeanors by severity, from most to least serious — the exact grading system varies by state.

ClassTypical examples
Class AAssault causing injury, fraudulent use of someone's credit card or ID info, allowing a child around drugs, shoplifting, vandalism, resisting arrest
Class BFirst-offense DWI, indecent exposure, failure to pay child support, minor drug possession (up to 2 oz), harassment, filing a false police report, criminal trespass or mischief (breaking and entering)
Class CTraffic tickets, public intoxication, simple assault, criminal trespassing, gambling, bail jumping, leaving a child in a vehicle, possessing alcohol in a vehicle, minor DUI

A charge only counts as a conviction once it's been adjudicated — the same term from drug testing applies here too, meaning the court has reached a final decision. A pending, unadjudicated charge reads differently on a report than a conviction does.

Drug testing (DT), panel by panel

A drug test is a lab analysis of a urine specimen to detect the presence or absence of specific drugs. Which panel a role needs depends entirely on the client and the position:

PanelScreens for
5-panelMarijuana, opiates, PCP, cocaine, amphetamines
7-panelThe 5-panel list, plus benzodiazepines and barbiturates
10-panelCocaine, marijuana, PCP, amphetamines, opiates, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, methadone, propoxyphene, and Quaaludes
12-panelExtends the 10-panel with additional prescription painkillers and other controlled substances of concern for the workplace

Most client requests land on a 10- or 12-panel test. Results generally take 24–72 hours, sometimes longer depending on the substance.

Any positive result is a red flag by default. If a candidate expects a positive result because of a legitimately prescribed medication, they need to disclose that before the test and provide a copy of their most recent prescription from their doctor. A Medical Review Officer (MRO) reviews prescription claims tied to a positive result as part of adjudication — the formal process of deciding how a flagged result gets resolved.

HonorVet works with several drug-test and background-check vendors depending on the client: LabCorp typically handles collection, while Exact, Sterling, Orsus, ScoutLogic, and First Advantage serve as client portals where a submission shows as "completed" once the candidate has initiated the test — NY Best is used only for drug testing and medicals. Collection site hours vary by location (some accept walk-ins only within a narrow window, so always confirm the specific site's hours before sending a candidate).

A note on marijuana specifically: as of 2026 it's legal for recreational use in 24 states plus D.C., and for medical use in around 40 states — but it remains a Schedule I controlled substance under federal law. That federal status is generally what governs client drug policies, so a positive marijuana result can still count as positive even in a state where its use is legal, unless the specific client says otherwise.
